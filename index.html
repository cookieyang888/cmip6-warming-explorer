MOTIVATION & RESEARCH QUESTION

Project motivation and research question

A single temperature trend can hide how warming differs across places and within the year. We wanted viewers to compare the selected emissions scenarios while also seeing whether the resulting changes are concentrated in particular months.

Our question is: How do emissions scenarios shape warming across locations and seasons throughout the 21st century? We focus on four selected locations to keep the exploration manageable while allowing comparisons across different modeled seasonal patterns.

Our six checkpoint graphs examined annual trajectories, 20-year trailing averages, differences across locations, and monthly temperature changes. The annual graphs showed why multi-year comparisons are useful, while the monthly graphs revealed seasonal variation that annual means can hide.

Those observations led to the three linked views at the top of this page. Users can choose among four locations, select one or more scenarios, and choose a time period, then compare the annual change with its seasonal pattern and the changes at other locations.

DATA & METHOD

Data sources and calculations

What you are exploring

We use CMIP6 output from NCAR’s CESM2 model. The variable tas is near-surface air temperature, and Amon identifies monthly atmosphere output. We use member r4i1p1f1 (one ensemble run of the model) on gn (the native model grid). We compare SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 for 2015–2100. The four locations are illustrative choices, not a representative sample of the world.

From model output to temperature change

For each location, we select the nearest latitude and longitude grid cell, using circular longitude distance at 0°/360°. We convert Kelvin to Celsius by subtracting 273.15. Annual means weight monthly temperatures by the number of days in each model-calendar month. The annual baseline is the mean of 30 annual values from the historical simulation for 1985–2014; the future scenario simulations begin in 2015. A temperature anomaly means a change relative to this baseline.

What changes when you brush

The preset periods are Near term (2021–2040), Mid century (2041–2060), and Late century (2081–2100). The brush and year inputs also allow custom periods within 2015–2100.

The seasonal chart averages each calendar month across your selected years, then subtracts that month’s historical 1985–2014 average. Summary values and the location comparison average annual anomalies over the selected years. The optional 20-year trailing average is dated at the final year of its window; its first complete future window ends in 2034. Changing that trend display does not change the linked calculations.

Data limitations

These results come from one member of one model. They do not show ensemble uncertainty or observed city temperatures. Coarse cells can include land and water and do not represent urban heat. Local changes relative to 1985–2014 are not the Paris Agreement’s global preindustrial thresholds. Short selections retain modeled year-to-year variability.

DESIGN & INTERACTION

Chart design and linked interactions

Why these views

Our exploratory analysis showed differences across scenarios, locations and calendar months. Line charts use horizontal position for time and vertical position for temperature change, allowing viewers to follow trajectories and seasonal patterns. Horizontal bars put period averages on a common positional scale for comparing places.

Teal, ochre and red consistently identify the same scenarios in all three views. Line dashes and text labels provide additional cues. The charts keep fixed vertical temperature scales across filters so changes in scale cannot exaggerate a comparison. Zero marks the historical baseline; values below zero indicate cooler conditions relative to that baseline.

Why the interaction matters

The brush connects a user-chosen period to its seasonal pattern and regional comparison. This supports questions such as whether a large annual increase is concentrated in particular months, and whether the same scenario produces different changes elsewhere. Presets and numeric year controls offer alternative ways to select time. Tooltips and the expandable data table expose exact values; reset restores the initial view.

Displaying every location and scenario together would create overlapping lines. Small multiples offer simultaneous comparisons but require many panels to cover arbitrary periods. Our design combines a focused location view with a linked comparison across places.

DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

From six exploratory graphs
to three connected views.

We began with a shared CMIP6 dataset and divided the checkpoint analysis into time trends, location comparisons and seasonal changes. We used the resulting six graphs to develop the research question, then brought those comparisons together in a D3 site hosted on GitHub Pages.

David Yang

Collected and prepared the CMIP6 data, documented the data transformations, and developed and deployed the interactive website. Integrated the team’s exploratory analysis into the final visualization.

Eric Che

Created the two exploratory graphs examining annual temperature anomalies and 20-year trailing averages in San Diego. Interpreted the scenario differences and helped refine the research question.

Nam Ngo

Created the two exploratory graphs comparing warming across locations and emissions scenarios. Summarized regional differences and helped check the numerical findings and their interpretation.

Jun Tao Wang

Created the two exploratory graphs examining seasonal temperature changes. Documented the monthly comparison method and explained patterns that annual averages could hide.

All members contributed to the checkpoint preparation and discussion of the project’s direction. The website combines annual values and the 20-year trailing average in one chart, links the seasonal chart to a selected time range, and adds a comparison of period averages across locations.

Time and development effort

We estimate about 5 hours per member, or about 20 person-hours in total. This includes data preparation, exploratory analysis, project discussions, checkpoint preparation, and website implementation and deployment.

Website development and deployment were the most time-consuming parts of the project. This work included connecting the D3 views so that changes in location, scenario or time period update the related charts and numerical summaries, arranging the page for different screen sizes, and publishing the static files through GitHub Pages. We also checked the website’s controls, data downloads, mobile layout and selected numerical results before deployment.
